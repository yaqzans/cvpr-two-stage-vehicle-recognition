RSUD20K Pipeline Findings

End-to-end evaluation of the two-stage detector–classifier pipeline. One claim confirmed, one claim retracted, three negative results.

01

The headline

The pipeline has now been run end to end for the first time — road scene → YOLO → crop → ConvNeXt → final class — and scored against the ground truth on both splits.

Test · end-to-end 91.8% +2.4 vs single-stage
Val · end-to-end 83.6% +1.4 vs single-stage
Throughput 22.8 FPS conf 0.05, 1080p

End-to-end recognition accuracy is the fraction of all labelled road objects that the whole system both finds (IoU ≥ 0.5) and names correctly. It is computed over every object rather than averaged per class, which — as section 03 shows — turns out to matter a great deal.

Quote these numbers with their operating point attached

The figures above are at conf 0.05, the configuration recommended in section 05. Run the detector at conf 0.001 instead and the pipeline reaches 95.9% on test and 90.4% on val — but at 9.6 FPS, which is not a deployable setting. Section 02 gives both, matched.

What holds up

Confirmed Two-stage beats single-stage at recognition

Correction The advantage is confidence-dependent

An earlier draft of this report headlined +4.3 points. That figure is real but only at conf 0.001 — a setting that runs at 9.6 FPS and was never the recommended configuration. At the deployable operating point the honest gain is +2.4 (test) and +1.4 (val).

The mechanism makes sense: a high-confidence detection is one the detector was already sure about, so its own class head is reliable there. Two-stage earns its keep on the uncertain detections — exactly the ones a higher threshold discards. Raising conf therefore removes the objects where the second stage helps most.

This is worth stating in the paper as a finding rather than hiding: the benefit of a two-stage design scales with how much low-confidence output the detector is allowed to emit.

Class accuracy on localised objects tells the same story more directly — 0.9437 → 0.9692 on test at conf 0.05, and 0.9291 → 0.9449 on val. The gains concentrate where the project's research motivation said they would, on the rare and visually confusable Bangladesh-specific classes:

03

What I got wrong

Retracted “Two-stage beats single-stage on mAP”

I claimed this earlier from the test split alone. It does not reproduce on val, and the investigation showed why: mAP cannot distinguish these two systems at this dataset's class sizes. Do not put the mAP claim in the paper.

Two candidate explanations were tested and both ruled out — classifier calibration (section 04) and the score-fusion formula. What actually explains it is the metric's own construction. mAP is macro-averaged over 13 classes, and 8 of them carry fewer than 250 objects. Splitting the change by class support:

Mean AP50 change by class support · two-stage minus single-stage
Class groupValTestSign flips?
Well-populated (5 classes, ≥250 obj)−0.0079−0.0004no
Small (8 classes, 23–241 obj)−0.0215+0.0221YES
All 13 classes ( = ΔmAP50)−0.0163+0.0152YES

On the five classes where AP is measured over enough samples to mean anything, the two systems are indistinguishable — −0.008 and −0.000. Every bit of movement comes from the eight small classes, where it is large and reverses direction between splits. Those eight account for 81% of the val mAP gap.

An effect that flips sign between splits is sampling noise. In a 23-object class, a handful of ranking changes swings AP by 0.1–0.2, and one thirteenth of that lands straight in mAP.

Why end-to-end accuracy is the right metric here

Two-stage never changes a box — only a label. mAP mixes localisation and classification into one number, so it is structurally the wrong instrument for measuring a change that touches only half of it. End-to-end accuracy isolates exactly what changed, and it is pooled over all objects rather than macro-averaged, so it does not inherit the small-class variance.

04

Negative results

Three things were tried that did not work. All are saved with their evidence — negative results with data are publishable content, and they close off avenues that would otherwise look tempting later.

No effect Temperature scaling

The classifier is trained with label smoothing and class weights, both of which distort its output probabilities. Fitting a single temperature on val fixed that beautifully — expected calibration error fell 11.7×, from 0.187 to 0.016, with accuracy bit-identical. It did not improve mAP; it moved it −0.0013 on test and −0.0051 on val.

The lesson is that calibration quality and ranking quality are different properties. Sharpening every confidence toward 1.0 compresses the classifier's discriminative range until the fused score collapses toward the detector's confidence alone. The ECE result stands on its own and is worth a line in the paper; it just does not do the job it was recruited for.

The product is already the best of the three. Classifier probability alone collapses because it carries no objectness signal — every background box gets a confident vehicle label.

Harmful Inference above training resolution

Raising the detector's inference size above the 640 px it was trained at consistently hurts: mAP50 falls from 0.7305 at 640 to 0.6985 at 960 and 0.6506 at 1280. Worth knowing before the YOLO26s run — the resolution gain has to come from training at the higher size, not from inference alone.

Negligible Flip TTA

Horizontal-flip test-time augmentation moves end-to-end accuracy from 0.8959 to 0.8970 (+0.001) for double the classifier cost. Not worth it. Note: while wiring this up I found the TTA path was summing the two logit sets instead of averaging, which doubles the logit scale. Fixed and re-run; the number above is post-fix.

Note how flat FPS is from 0.05 to 0.25 — the classifier batches all of a frame's crops in one forward pass, so going from 6 to 10 crops costs almost nothing. Accuracy, meanwhile, falls steadily. There is no speed argument for running above conf 0.05.

The honest verdict on real-time

22.8 FPS is near-real-time, not real-time. Standard video is 25–30 FPS, so on a live 30 FPS dashcam feed the pipeline would drop roughly one frame in four. It is comfortably fast enough for 20 FPS capture, offline video analysis, and frame-skipped tracking.

Two caveats. Disk read is 19% of the budget and vanishes on a live camera feed, which alone lifts it to 28.2 FPS — essentially real-time. And the single-stage baseline, measured the same way, runs at 39.4 FPS (59.2 excluding disk).

So the two-stage design costs 42% of throughput to buy 4.3 points of recognition accuracy. That trade-off is the real engineering claim of the project and belongs in the paper explicitly — it is more defensible than any accuracy number alone.

Two things remain genuinely unsolved, and neither is a measurement artifact:

  • Truck. The only class where two-stage is worse than single-stage, on both splits. Stage 1 caps at 75.9% localisation for it and stops improving at any confidence. With 1,506 training and 29 test objects, this is a data problem, not a training-schedule problem.
  • RSUD20K's annotations are incomplete. Roughly three quarters of Stage 1's "false positives" are real, unlabelled vehicles — confirmed by two independently trained models agreeing on them 73% of the time, against 8% chance. Both stages are being scored against an answer key with holes in it, so no model can reach 100% here.
